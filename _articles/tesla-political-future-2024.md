*Historical forecast reconstruction · September 20, 2024*

Elon Musk’s growing involvement in American politics has made Tesla an increasingly interesting company to follow. Since endorsing Donald Trump in July, Musk has become more involved in the presidential campaign, and earlier this month, Trump proposed establishing a government-efficiency commission headed by Musk if he returns to office.

I’ve been thinking about what this might mean for Tesla, particularly because the company is already quite unusual from an investment perspective. Its valuation isn’t based entirely on the cars it manufactures today. Investors are also paying for the possibility that autonomous driving, artificial intelligence, and energy storage could eventually become much larger parts of its business.

Musk’s political involvement adds another consideration. A change in government could affect electric-vehicle subsidies, environmental regulations, international trade, and potentially the rules surrounding autonomous vehicles. Some of these changes might benefit Tesla, while others could make its business more difficult.

What interests me is how much of Tesla’s valuation could eventually depend on these political developments, even if its actual financial performance doesn’t change very much.

## Tesla’s business and its valuation are telling different stories

Tesla’s recent financial results provide a useful starting point. The company [delivered 443,956 vehicles](https://ir.tesla.com/press-release/tesla-vehicle-production-deliveries-and-date-financial-results-webcast-second-quarter-2024) in the second quarter of 2024, approximately 5% fewer than during the same period last year.

![Tesla quarterly vehicle deliveries](tesla-deliveries-2024.png)

*Tesla vehicle deliveries, Q2 2023 and Q2 2024. Source: Tesla investor relations.*

Its automotive business has also been facing pressure from lower selling prices and growing competition. According to [Tesla’s latest quarterly filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1318605/000162828024032662/tsla-20240630.htm), automotive gross margin declined from 19.2% to 18.5% compared with the previous year.

These aren’t particularly encouraging figures, although they don’t suggest that Tesla is in serious financial difficulty either. The company remains an established manufacturer with considerable resources, a recognizable brand, and a growing energy-storage business.

One detail I find especially interesting is the role of regulatory credits in Tesla’s profitability.

Tesla earns these credits by producing vehicles that help other manufacturers meet emissions requirements. In the second quarter, it generated $890 million from regulatory credits, compared with $282 million a year earlier.

![Tesla automotive regulatory credit revenue](tesla-regulatory-credits-2024.png)

*Automotive regulatory-credit revenue, USD millions. Source: Tesla Q2 2024 Form 10-Q.*

That’s a substantial increase, and it helped support the company’s automotive margins at a time when vehicle prices were under pressure.

It also creates an interesting contradiction. Tesla benefits financially from environmental regulations, yet Musk is supporting a presidential candidate who has expressed opposition to several policies intended to encourage electric-vehicle adoption.

Of course, opposing consumer subsidies isn’t necessarily the same as opposing emissions regulations. These policies operate differently, and changes to one wouldn’t automatically affect the others.

Still, I think the distinction is important when considering whether a change in government would actually benefit Tesla.

## The economics of Musk’s political involvement

In August, [Trump said he would consider eliminating the $7,500 federal tax credit](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/trump-says-he-would-consider-ending-7500-electric-vehicle-credit-3578172) available to qualifying electric-vehicle buyers. He also indicated that he would consider giving Musk an advisory role.

Removing the tax credit would make eligible electric vehicles more expensive for consumers, assuming manufacturers didn’t fully offset the change through lower prices. That could reduce demand for Tesla’s vehicles, particularly when affordability is already an important consideration.

But there’s another side to the argument.

Tesla has established manufacturing operations, a strong brand, and more financial flexibility than many smaller EV manufacturers. If government support were reduced, some competitors might find it harder to remain competitive.

Tesla could therefore be negatively affected by the policy while still performing better than certain rivals.

I think this is a reasonable possibility, although it’s easy to exaggerate. Traditional automakers have substantial resources of their own, and Tesla also benefits from government incentives. A policy that weakens its competitors doesn’t necessarily make Tesla more profitable.

The political relationship becomes more interesting when considering autonomous driving.

Tesla has invested heavily in its Full Self-Driving technology and has discussed plans to develop autonomous transportation services. If successful, these businesses could eventually change how investors value the company.

However, Tesla’s current Full Self-Driving system still requires driver supervision. Developing a commercially viable driverless service involves technical, regulatory, and operational challenges that cannot simply be resolved through a change in government.

A future administration might approach autonomous-vehicle regulation differently, and investors could interpret that as an improvement in Tesla’s prospects. But regulatory flexibility wouldn’t guarantee technological success, nor would Musk’s political involvement automatically translate into favorable treatment.

This is where I think the distinction between Tesla’s business and its share price becomes particularly important.

A company’s valuation reflects expectations about future earnings, not just its current financial results. If investors become more optimistic about Tesla’s autonomous-driving ambitions, its share price could rise before those businesses generate meaningful profits.

That doesn’t necessarily mean the company has become more valuable in a fundamental sense. It may simply mean investors have become more willing to pay for an uncertain future.

## China makes the picture more complicated

There’s another part of Tesla’s business that makes the political argument less straightforward.

Tesla is an American company, but it operates internationally and faces increasingly capable competitors in China. Manufacturers such as BYD have developed considerable advantages in production costs, battery supply chains, and competitive pricing.

The United States has announced substantial tariffs on Chinese electric vehicles, while the European Union is pursuing additional duties on vehicles manufactured in China.

At first glance, these restrictions might appear beneficial for Tesla because they make it harder for Chinese competitors to enter certain markets.

But Tesla also manufactures vehicles in Shanghai.

In August, the [European Commission proposed an additional duty of 9%](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/api/files/document/print/en/ip_24_4301/IP_24_4301_EN.pdf) on Tesla vehicles imported from China, although this was lower than the rates proposed for several Chinese manufacturers.

![Proposed EU additional duties](eu-ev-duties-proposed-aug2024.png)

*Draft additional EU duty rates announced in August 2024, subject to revision; excludes the ordinary 10% tariff.*

That creates an unusual situation. Trade restrictions intended to protect domestic manufacturers could also increase costs for Tesla’s international operations.

And even if tariffs make Chinese vehicles less competitive in the United States or Europe, they don’t eliminate the competition Tesla faces in China and other markets.

I don’t think American trade policy alone can solve that problem.

Chinese manufacturers have become competitive for reasons that extend beyond government support, and those advantages aren’t going to disappear simply because importing their vehicles becomes more expensive.

## What I think happens next

My expectation is that Tesla’s share price will become increasingly sensitive to political developments as the November election approaches.

I don’t necessarily expect that to translate into a sustained increase in the stock. Tesla’s automotive business faces real challenges, and some proposed policy changes could make those challenges more difficult.

But I think investors may begin paying greater attention to the potential value of Tesla’s future businesses, particularly autonomous driving, and how different regulatory environments might affect their development.

This is an important distinction because Tesla doesn’t need to demonstrate an immediate improvement in profitability for its valuation to change. Investors only need to revise their expectations about what the company might earn in the future.

Whether those expectations eventually prove justified is another question.

I also think there’s a risk in attaching too much importance to Musk’s political relationships. His interests and Tesla’s commercial interests aren’t necessarily identical, and political involvement can create uncertainty just as easily as it creates opportunities.

Still, I find it difficult to ignore how closely Tesla’s investment story is becoming connected to developments outside its existing automotive business.

The company will continue being judged on how many cars it sells and how profitably it manufactures them. But I suspect its share price will increasingly reflect expectations about technologies and regulatory developments whose economic value remains uncertain.

For a company already valued partly on what it might become, politics could add another layer to that uncertainty.

And that may be what makes Tesla particularly interesting to follow over the coming months.
