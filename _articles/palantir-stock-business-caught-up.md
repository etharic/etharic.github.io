I've been following Palantir's performance over the past few months, and its rise in the stock market has been difficult to ignore. Earlier this month, a [Barron's article](https://www.barrons.com/articles/palantir-stock-record-high-996483aa) reported that the company's shares had reached another record high, having gained more than 130% since the beginning of the year. Its recent inclusion in the S&P 500, along with growing excitement around artificial intelligence, has given investors plenty of reasons to pay attention. What interests me, though, is how quickly expectations around the company seem to have changed. Palantir has certainly made progress as a business, particularly in its commercial operations, but its share price has increased much faster than its revenue. That isn't necessarily unusual, since investors value companies based on what they expect them to earn in the future rather than just what they're earning today. Still, I think Palantir is an interesting example of how a company can become more successful without necessarily becoming a better investment at its current price.

## A business that's making real progress

Palantir has traditionally been associated with government and intelligence work. Its software helps organizations bring together information from different sources, analyze it, and make better use of it. More recently, the company has been expanding into the commercial market, particularly through its Artificial Intelligence Platform, or AIP. Its latest financial results have been encouraging. According to [Palantir's second-quarter earnings announcement](https://investors.palantir.com/news-details/2024/Palantir-Reports-Revenue-Growth-of-27-Year-Over-Year-and-Raises-Full-Year-Revenue-Guidance-Record-GAAP-EPS-of-0.06-in-Q2-2024/), revenue grew 27% from the previous year to approximately $678 million, while US commercial revenue grew even faster, increasing 55% to $159 million. I find the commercial growth particularly interesting because it suggests that Palantir's software could have a much wider market than government agencies alone. However, US commercial revenue still accounts for less than a quarter of the company's total revenue, so it would be a mistake to assume the entire business is growing at the same pace.

Profitability has improved as well. Palantir reported around $105 million in operating income and $134 million in net income attributable to common shareholders during the quarter. Part of the difference came from interest earned on its cash holdings rather than its software operations, which is worth keeping in mind when assessing the company's profitability. Nevertheless, Palantir is growing while making money from its core business, giving investors something more substantial to work with than expectations about future growth alone. I think this is an important improvement because it suggests that the company is beginning to demonstrate the financial potential of its technology, rather than relying entirely on what it might achieve several years from now.

## What investors are paying for

On October 25, Palantir closed at approximately $44.86 per share, giving it a market value of around $100 billion. With the company expecting roughly $2.75 billion in revenue this year, investors are paying about 36 times its expected annual sales. That's a high valuation, although it doesn't automatically mean the stock is overpriced. Software companies can sometimes justify these valuations because they often have long-term customer relationships and can grow without their costs increasing at the same rate. Palantir's software can require considerable work to set up and maintain, but the company could still become more profitable as it grows. If revenue continues increasing while profit margins improve, earnings could grow much faster than sales, particularly if its AI products attract more commercial customers over the coming years.

I can understand why investors find that possibility attractive, but what makes me cautious is how much future success already seems to be reflected in the share price. At around 36 times expected revenue, Palantir needs to deliver substantial growth over several years to support its valuation. Even if the company performs well, its shares could disappoint if that performance falls short of what investors already expect. That's the part I find most difficult about valuing companies like Palantir. Recognizing that a business has good prospects is relatively straightforward, but deciding how much those prospects are worth today is considerably harder. The price investors pay matters just as much as the quality of the company they're investing in.

## Politics and the defense technology opportunity

Palantir's political connections have also attracted attention as the US presidential election approaches. Peter Thiel, one of the company's co-founders, has longstanding ties within Republican political circles. JD Vance, Donald Trump's running mate, previously worked at Thiel's investment firm and received substantial financial support from him during his Senate campaign. Given Palantir's work with government agencies, these relationships are worth understanding, but I wouldn't place too much importance on them when evaluating the company. Political connections don't guarantee government contracts, and Palantir's interests aren't necessarily the same as those of its founders. Its CEO, Alex Karp, has also publicly supported Kamala Harris, which makes it difficult to describe the company as politically aligned with one candidate.

What I find more interesting is the growing importance of software in defense and intelligence. Modern military operations rely heavily on collecting and analyzing information, and Palantir already has experience building software for these purposes. That experience could help it compete for future projects, although government contracts can be difficult to win and take time to secure. Higher defense spending doesn't necessarily mean every company working in the industry will benefit equally, either. For me, Palantir's existing products and customer relationships are more convincing reasons to follow its government business than speculation about political influence.

## A good business at a demanding price

Palantir's recent [inclusion in the S&P 500](https://press.spglobal.com/2024-09-06-Palantir-Technologies%2C-Dell-Technologies%2C-and-Erie-Indemnity-Set-to-Join-S-P-500-Others-to-Join-S-P-MidCap-400-and-S-P-SmallCap-600) is another sign of how much the company has progressed. It has brought greater visibility and required index-tracking funds to add its shares, although this doesn't directly improve the underlying business. There are good reasons to be optimistic about Palantir. Its revenue growth has improved, it's generating operating profits, and its commercial business gives it opportunities beyond government work. At the same time, its valuation requires investors to have considerable confidence that this growth will continue, which is why I think the company's progress and its share price need to be considered separately.

Palantir could continue growing and become much more profitable over the next several years, and I wouldn't be surprised if it does. But that doesn't necessarily mean its stock will deliver equally impressive returns from today's valuation. A company can perform well and still turn out to be a disappointing investment if much of its future success has already been priced in. For now, I'm more convinced by Palantir's business progress than I am by its share price, although I don't think there's enough evidence to conclude that investors are necessarily wrong. Sometimes the difficult part of investing isn't finding a promising business, but figuring out how much of that promise has already been reflected in the price.
