*Silver’s industrial demand and supply characteristics provide a basis for relative outperformance, although investment flows remain the key variable.*

## Investment view

Silver presents an interesting relative-value opportunity following gold’s strong performance over the past year. With the gold-to-silver ratio approaching 90 at the end of January, silver appears relatively inexpensive compared with gold across several historical periods. While this does not establish fundamental undervaluation, resilient industrial demand and relatively slow supply adjustment provide a reasonable basis for expecting stronger price performance if investment interest in precious metals continues.

The central expectation is for silver to outperform gold in percentage terms over the twelve months ending February 2026. This view is primarily driven by the potential for additional investment demand rather than an anticipated shortage of physical silver.

## Market context

Gold extended its rally at the end of January, with [Reuters reporting](https://www.kitco.com/news/off-the-wire/2025-01-31/gold-surges-past-2800-tariff-threats-reignite-record-rally) that spot prices had exceeded $2,800 per troy ounce for the first time. Demand for defensive assets has remained strong despite elevated bond yields, supported by economic uncertainty and investors seeking diversification.

Silver has participated in the broader precious-metals rally but has not matched gold’s recent performance. At approximately $31–32 per ounce, the gold-to-silver ratio stood close to 90 at the end of January. The ratio is a useful measure of relative pricing, although differences in liquidity, demand composition and economic sensitivity mean that historical comparisons cannot establish an appropriate valuation on their own.

The potential opportunity lies in how silver responds to incremental investment demand. Its smaller market can experience larger percentage price movements when investor positioning changes, making a narrowing of the ratio plausible if precious-metal investment flows broaden beyond gold.

\### Sensitivity to changes in the gold-to-silver ratio

Silver price = Gold price / Gold-to-silver ratio. Assuming gold remains at $2,800 per ounce:

| Ratio | Implied silver price | Return from $31.50 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 90 | $31.11 | −1.2% |
| 85 | $32.94 | +4.6% |
| 80 | $35.00 | +11.1% |
| 75 | $37.33 | +18.5% |

Illustrative sensitivity only; these are not price targets. Gold is fixed at $2,800 per ounce and silver reference price is $31.50.

A narrowing of the ratio to 80 would imply a silver price of $35 under these assumptions, approximately 11% above the reference price. This illustrates relative repricing, not a forecast of the future ratio.

## Industrial demand and supply fundamentals

Silver’s industrial applications provide an additional source of demand that distinguishes it from gold. The metal is widely used in electronics, electrical equipment and photovoltaic systems, where its conductivity and other physical properties make it an important manufacturing input.

The [Silver Institute’s January 2025 outlook](https://silverinstitute.org/global-silver-market-forecast-to-remain-in-a-sizeable-deficit-in-2025/) forecasts industrial demand growth of approximately 3% in 2025, exceeding 700 million ounces for the first time. Total demand, however, is expected to remain broadly stable at approximately 1.20 billion ounces, as weaker jewelry and silverware consumption offsets part of the industrial increase. This suggests that industrial growth may provide support without materially tightening aggregate demand.

Supply is expected to increase by approximately 3% to 1.05 billion ounces, leaving a projected annual deficit of around 149 million ounces. The deficit would mark the fifth consecutive year in which demand exceeds newly available supply, although it is smaller than the estimated shortfall for 2024.

A substantial proportion of silver is produced as a by-product of mining other metals, limiting the extent to which higher silver prices can stimulate additional mine output in the short term. However, existing inventories and recycling can help absorb market imbalances. The projected deficit should therefore be viewed as a potential source of market sensitivity rather than evidence of an immediate physical shortage.

## Outlook and risks

The case for silver outperformance depends on investment demand strengthening while industrial consumption remains broadly resilient. Under these conditions, limited short-term supply flexibility could increase the price impact of additional buying, potentially allowing silver to appreciate faster than gold.

The principal risk is weaker global manufacturing activity. Unlike gold, silver has substantial exposure to industrial demand, making it more vulnerable to a slowdown in production. Higher real interest rates or a stronger US dollar could also reduce investor demand for precious metals.

Silver consequently offers greater potential for relative appreciation alongside higher downside volatility. The elevated gold-to-silver ratio provides a useful starting point for the analysis, but the direction of investment flows and the resilience of industrial demand will ultimately determine whether the opportunity materializes.

The balance of these factors supports a constructive twelve-month view on silver relative to gold, without requiring a significant change in gold’s own investment outlook.
