I've been meaning to start a personal website for six years. I know this because I recently found a journal entry from six years ago explaining, in surprising detail, why starting one would be a good idea. Apparently, I was quite convincing. Just not convincing enough to get myself to actually do it.

At the time, I didn't have the resources, or perhaps the resolve, to follow through. And as these things usually go, life got busy, other interests took over, and the idea quietly moved to the back of my mind. Reading that entry again was a little uncomfortable. Six years is a long time to keep putting off something you still believe is worth doing.

I've spent a good part of those years reading people like **Paul Graham**, **Derek Sivers**, **Sam Altman**, and **Ryan Kulp**. Their personal websites have been some of my favorite corners of the internet, and a big part of why I've wanted one of my own. They've shown me how much you can learn from someone who's willing to share their thinking openly, without needing a camera, a large audience, or much more than a place to write.

More recently, the youtuber **Eric Reysu's** writing and YouTube videos gave me the final push. Watching him consistently share what he's learning, building, and experimenting with made the whole thing feel much more approachable. So I finally decided to make one.

There are three reasons I think it's worth doing.

## increasing the surface area of luck

I've always liked the idea of increasing the *surface area of luck*. A lot of good things in life happen because you happen to meet the right person, come across the right idea, or find yourself in the right place at the right time. You can't plan most of these things, but you can make it easier for them to happen.

A few decades ago, if you had an idea worth sharing, your audience was largely limited to the people around you. Reaching strangers meant getting published, finding an audience through an institution, or paying for advertising. None of those options was particularly accessible. Today, you can write something from your bedroom, put it on a website, and someone on the other side of the world might find it years later. It's easy to take that for granted now, but I think it's one of the more extraordinary opportunities the internet has given us.

Of course, putting something online doesn't mean people will read it. Most things we publish will probably go unnoticed. But the possibility exists, and the cost of trying is almost nothing.

YouTube offers similar opportunities, but making videos comes with its own demands. You need to be comfortable on camera, record, edit, and think about how everything looks and sounds. I admire people who do it well, but writing feels like a much easier place for me to start.

I've personally benefited from strangers making that choice. When I started learning Japanese, I came across a blog called **All Japanese All the Time**, written by someone who went by **Khatzumoto**. He had taught himself Japanese through an unusually intensive approach to immersion and decided to share what he'd learned. Some of his ideas changed how I approached language learning. I had never met him, and he had no idea I existed. Yet something he wrote helped a stranger years later.

Another example I love is **Tim Urban's** **Wait But Why**. Tim writes long, thoughtful explanations of subjects he wants to understand, often accompanied by wonderfully simple drawings. His curiosity eventually led to an unexpected invitation from **Elon Musk** to learn about Tesla and SpaceX firsthand.

That's an extraordinary outcome, and certainly not one I'm expecting. But it illustrates something interesting: writing about what genuinely interests you can lead to opportunities you couldn't have anticipated. Maybe something I write will help someone. Maybe it'll introduce me to a future friend, colleague, or mentor. Maybe nothing unusual will happen at all. I'd still rather have those possibilities than not.

## a modern-day business card

The second reason is a little more practical. I think a personal website is a modern-day business card, or perhaps a more complete version of a résumé.

A résumé tells people where you've studied, where you've worked, and what you've accomplished. It's useful, but it leaves out most of the things that make someone interesting. It doesn't tell you what they spend their weekends learning, which problems they find fascinating, what they've tried and failed at, or how their interests have changed over time. A personal website can.

People sometimes call this building a personal brand, although I prefer to think of it as giving people a more complete picture of who I am and what interests me.

I've decided to call this little corner of the internet **Etharic**. There's something satisfying about giving what you do a name of its own, separate from your own. It makes the whole thing feel like something you're building rather than simply a collection of things you've done. Even small bits of progress, whether on a project or in my personal life, feel a little more meaningful when they become part of something that can grow over time. I suppose there's a quiet sense of ownership in that, too.

I want this to be a place where I can share ideas, keep track of my year, and write detailed guides to projects I've built or adopted. Some will be technical. Others might have to do with finance, learning, or something completely unrelated that happens to catch my attention. Not everything will be particularly impressive, and that's fine.

The value, at least to me, is in having these things together, presented in a way that feels like my own. A record of what I've spent my time thinking about and doing.

I've also come to appreciate how much of life is shaped by conversations. I've always enjoyed striking up conversations with people, including those whose work has little to do with mine. Over the years, I've met people working in finance, technology, law, and entrepreneurship, sometimes in the most ordinary circumstances.

Looking back, I can trace quite a few changes in my life to conversations with people who were generous enough to share their experiences with me. Some introduced me to new interests, while others changed how I approached things I already cared about. I owe quite a lot to those conversations.

In a way, I see this website as an extension of them. Instead of waiting to meet someone before exchanging ideas, I can put a few of mine here. Someone with similar interests might stumble across them. And if that leads to an interesting conversation someday, all the better.

## the satisfaction of making something useful

The final reason is probably the most important to me. I spend a lot of time consuming things: books, articles, videos, podcasts, and conversations. There's so much good material available that you could spend your entire life learning from other people and never run out.

But I've spent years learning from things other people have made. I'd like to spend a little more time on the other side of that exchange, contributing something of my own rather than always being the one consuming it.

Writing is one way of doing that. It forces me to take ideas that seem perfectly clear in my head and find out whether I actually understand them. Quite often, I suspect I'll discover that I don't. That's useful in itself.

There's also a different kind of satisfaction in making something that helps another person.

One of the activities I find most meaningful is volunteering with children in institutional care. Much of what I do involves helping them with learning and education, whether that's explaining a difficult concept, working through an exercise, or simply finding an approach that makes something easier to understand.

The children I work with are capable and curious. What they haven't always had are the same circumstances and opportunities as others. I don't think of my role as doing anything extraordinary. Often, it's just about being present, patient, and useful.

What stays with me are the small moments when something finally makes sense, or when a conversation helps someone see a problem differently. Those moments are surprisingly fulfilling. I'd like this website to carry a little of that same spirit.

Obviously, writing something online is not the same as sitting beside someone and helping them work through a problem. But if I learn something useful, find a better way of doing something, or spend weeks figuring out a project, perhaps writing it down can save somebody else a little time.

It doesn't need to reach thousands of people to be worthwhile. If one person finds an explanation helpful, avoids a mistake I made, or discovers something they hadn't considered before, that's already a good reason to have written it. And even if nobody reads a particular post, I'll have a record of what I was thinking and learning at that point in my life. That seems worthwhile, too.

## a place to begin

I don't have a grand plan for this website. For now, I want to write regularly, document the things I'm working on, and leave behind useful notes for whoever might find them. Some posts will be carefully researched guides. Others will simply be thoughts I'm still trying to make sense of.

I expect my interests to change, and I hope the website changes with them. Six years ago, I wrote down a few reasons to start something like this. I'm glad I eventually came back to them.

If you're curious about any of the same things, I hope you'll enjoy reading along. And if something here makes even a small part of someone's life easier, I'll consider it time well spent.
