*EA’s recurring revenue and cash-generative franchises support leveraged ownership, but the substantial equity requirement makes operating growth and exit valuation central to sponsor returns.*

## Executive assessment

The proposed acquisition of Electronic Arts by the Public Investment Fund (PIF), Silver Lake and Affinity Partners represents a significant test of leveraged-buyout economics at an unusually large transaction size.

The consortium has agreed to acquire EA for $210 per share in cash, with an announced transaction valuation of approximately $55 billion. Financing includes approximately $36 billion of equity commitments, incorporating PIF’s existing shareholding, and $20 billion of committed debt financing, of which $18 billion is expected to be funded at closing.

EA’s established franchises and recurring live-service revenue provide a relatively stable operating foundation. However, the acquisition is being undertaken at approximately 19 times estimated forward EBITDA, leaving limited room for earnings underperformance or exit multiple compression.

Our provisional analysis indicates that EA could support meaningful debt reduction under a reasonable range of cash-generation assumptions. Nevertheless, even an improving operating business may produce modest sponsor returns given the size of the initial equity commitment. The transaction’s economics are therefore likely to depend on the consortium’s investment horizon and return requirements, rather than conventional financial leverage alone.

## 1. Transaction terms and funding structure

EA’s September 29 announcement established the principal terms of the acquisition. The $210 consideration represents a premium of approximately 25% to EA’s unaffected closing price on September 25. The transaction remains subject to shareholder approval, regulatory review and customary closing conditions.

### Table 1. Announced transaction terms

| Item | Announced amount |
| --- | --- |
| Headline transaction valuation | ~$55.0bn |
| Consideration per share | $210 |
| Premium to unaffected price | ~25% |
| Equity commitments, including PIF rollover | ~$36bn |
| Committed debt financing | $20bn |
| Expected funded debt at closing | ~$18bn |
| Existing PIF ownership | 9.9% |
| Expected closing | Q1 FY2027 |

*Source: EA acquisition announcement, September 29, 2025. Rounded figures; not a reconciled sources-and-uses statement.*

The financing structure requires particular care. PIF’s existing shareholding is being rolled into the acquisition vehicle, meaning the disclosed equity commitment is not entirely new cash contributed at closing. Similarly, the difference between $20 billion of committed debt and $18 billion expected to be funded should not be interpreted as available operating liquidity.

EA reported approximately $2.25 billion of cash and short-term investments and approximately $1.9 billion of debt at March 31, 2025. These balances provide historical context, but the closing balance sheet will depend on intervening operations, debt treatment, minimum cash requirements and transaction expenses.

A fully reconciled sources-and-uses statement cannot be established from the headline announcement alone. For the return analysis, we therefore use $36 billion as a provisional aggregate equity-capital reference, consistent with the disclosed equity commitment, while recognizing that final funded equity and the economic treatment of PIF’s rollover may differ.

## 2. Operating performance and cash-flow quality

EA’s financial characteristics are relevant to its suitability for leveraged ownership. Its portfolio includes established sports franchises, recurring player spending and digital content sales, which provide revenue beyond initial game purchases. However, live-service revenue is not equivalent to contracted subscription revenue. Player engagement, release schedules and franchise performance remain important determinants of demand.

### Table 2. Historical performance and current outlook

| Metric | FY2024 actual | FY2025 actual | FY2026 guidance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Net revenue | 7.56 | 7.46 | 7.10–7.50 |
| Net bookings | 7.43 | 7.36 | 7.60–8.00 |
| Live services and other revenue | 5.55 | 5.46 | — |
| Operating cash flow | 2.32 | 2.08 | 2.20–2.40 |
| Capital expenditure | ~0.20 | ~0.22 | Not confirmed |
| Operating cash flow less capex | ~2.12 | ~1.86 | — |

*US$bn. Sources: EA FY2025 Form 10-K and July 29, 2025 FY2026 guidance. FY2026 figures are company forecasts.*

EA’s FY2026 outlook suggests improved operating cash generation despite relatively limited expected revenue growth. The difference between bookings and recognized revenue is particularly relevant because cash collections and accounting recognition may occur in different periods.

Using the $2.30 billion midpoint of FY2026 operating cash-flow guidance and approximately $0.23 billion of illustrative capital expenditure produces a preliminary cash-generation reference of $2.07 billion before adjusting for the acquisition’s new financing structure. This provides a starting point for the debt model, rather than an amount that can automatically be applied to debt repayment.

## 3. Entry valuation and financing burden

Reuters Breakingviews estimated EA’s FY2026 EBITDA at approximately $2.9 billion. Using the announced $55 billion transaction valuation as an approximate enterprise-value reference implies an entry multiple near 19×. This is a screening multiple, not a fully reconciled purchase-price calculation.

### Table 3. Financing-cost sensitivity

| Metric | 7% | 8% | 9% |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Annual interest on $18bn debt | 1.26 | 1.44 | 1.62 |
| Debt / FY2026E EBITDA | 6.2× | 6.2× | 6.2× |

*US$bn. Independent borrowing-cost assumptions, not disclosed financing terms.*

At $18 billion of expected funded acquisition debt, gross opening leverage would be approximately 6.2× forward EBITDA. The financing burden is substantial relative to EA’s historical cash generation. The company’s modest physical capital requirements are favorable, but game development, licensing and content expenditure remain economically necessary and are not fully represented by capital expenditure alone.

The transaction’s debt capacity must consequently be assessed using post-financing cash flow rather than EBITDA in isolation.

## 4. Provisional cash-sweep model

To address the limitations of the earlier LBO analysis, debt repayment is calculated from modeled cash availability. The model begins with $18 billion of acquisition debt and a $2.07 billion annual pre-acquisition-financing cash-flow reference. The latter grows at the same illustrative rate as EBITDA in each scenario.

### Table 4. Cash-sweep and deleveraging scenarios

| Metric | Downside | Base case | Upside |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Annual cash-flow growth | 3% | 6% | 9% |
| Opening acquisition debt | 18.0 | 18.0 | 18.0 |
| Year 1 cash available for repayment | 1.09 | 1.15 | 1.21 |
| Year 3 cash available for repayment | 1.35 | 1.57 | 1.80 |
| Year 5 cash available for repayment | 1.66 | 2.08 | 2.54 |
| Cumulative debt repayment | 6.82 | 7.95 | 9.17 |
| Ending debt | 11.18 | 10.05 | 8.83 |

*US$bn. Independent illustrative cash-sweep assumptions; not lender terms or company guidance.*

For the financing adjustment, we assume an 8% borrowing cost, a 25% effective cash-tax benefit on incremental interest, and approximately $45 million of historical interest expense already reflected in the starting cash-flow measure.

The model assumes all residual cash after this financing adjustment is used to repay debt. This is deliberately simplified: it does not incorporate minimum liquidity reserves, mandatory amortization, detailed working-capital schedules, debt fees, or restrictions on interest deductibility.

The base case indicates approximately $8 billion of cumulative debt reduction over five years, assuming cash generation grows by 6% annually and all modeled surplus is applied to repayment. This is more supportable than independently selecting an exit debt balance, but it remains conditional on the assumed cash-conversion profile.

## 5. Sponsor return analysis

The revised LBO scenarios use the cash-sweep outputs rather than separately assumed debt-repayment figures. For consistency, we apply a provisional $36 billion opening equity reference and a five-year holding period. Exit EBITDA is derived from the $2.9 billion forward reference using the scenario growth rates.

### Table 5. Five-year aggregate equity-return scenarios

| Metric | Downside | Base case | Upside |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| EBITDA CAGR | 3% | 6% | 9% |
| Exit EBITDA | 3.36 | 3.88 | 4.46 |
| Exit EV / EBITDA | 15× | 17× | 19× |
| Exit enterprise value | 50.4 | 66.0 | 84.7 |
| Ending modeled debt | 11.18 | 10.05 | 8.83 |
| Exit equity value | 39.2 | 55.9 | 75.9 |
| Gross equity MOIC | 1.09× | 1.55× | 2.11× |
| Five-year gross IRR | 1.7% | 9.2% | 16.1% |

*US$bn unless stated. Illustrative $36bn aggregate opening equity, five-year holding period; no interim distributions or fees.*

### Table 6. Required exit EBITDA at a 20% gross IRR hurdle

| Exit multiple | Required EBITDA |
| --- | --- |
| 17× | $5.86bn |
| 19× | $5.24bn |
| 21× | $4.74bn |

*Illustrative hurdle; $36bn opening equity, five-year holding period, $10bn exit net debt.*

The base case generates approximately 9% gross annualized equity returns. This is positive, but below a hypothetical 20% gross return hurdle sometimes used to assess leveraged-buyout opportunities. The upside case approaches 16%, requiring both stronger EBITDA growth and preservation of the approximate entry valuation multiple.

These results highlight the effect of the substantial initial equity commitment. Although leverage improves returns when enterprise value increases, the consortium is financing a large proportion of the acquisition with equity, limiting the extent to which financial engineering alone can generate high returns.

A five-year 20% gross IRR on a $36 billion equity investment requires approximately $89.6 billion of exit equity value. Assuming $10 billion of remaining net debt, the corresponding enterprise value requirement is approximately $99.6 billion. At a 19× exit multiple, required EBITDA of approximately $5.24 billion represents roughly 13% annualized growth from the $2.9 billion starting estimate.

## 6. Value creation and diligence priorities

EA’s existing franchises provide several potential avenues for growth, including higher player engagement, recurring monetization and broader licensing opportunities. However, the consortium has not disclosed a sufficiently detailed operating plan to justify assigning a specific synergy value.

Cost reductions should also be approached cautiously. Game development and intellectual-property investment support the franchises that make EA attractive in the first place. Reducing expenditure without considering its effect on future releases could improve short-term cash flow at the expense of longer-term value.

The most important areas for further diligence are franchise-level bookings, licensing renewals, cash conversion, development expenditure, debt covenants and financing terms.

EA’s FY2025 filing disclosed approximately $1.5 billion of commitments to content licensors, independent developers and distribution partners for which performance obligations remained outstanding. These commitments are not equivalent to financial debt, but their timing and economic implications are relevant to the cash-flow forecast.

The transaction also remains exposed to shareholder and regulatory approvals. The September 29 merger agreement contains provisions governing termination and alternative acquisition proposals, and the acquisition is not subject to a financing condition.

## 7. Transaction assessment

EA’s operating characteristics provide a credible basis for leveraged ownership. Its established franchises, recurring digital revenue and relatively modest physical capital requirements support cash generation, while the consortium’s equity commitments reduce dependence on acquisition debt.

The valuation is the more demanding element. At approximately 19× forward EBITDA, the acquisition requires continued operating performance and meaningful long-term earnings growth to produce attractive aggregate equity returns.

The integrated scenarios suggest that EA could reduce acquisition debt substantially under reasonable cash-generation assumptions. However, moderate EBITDA growth and deleveraging alone may not be sufficient to achieve conventional high-teens or 20% gross sponsor returns.

The transaction may nevertheless make sense for a consortium with differing strategic objectives and investment horizons. PIF’s existing gaming investments could support a longer-term ownership approach, while the financial sponsors may seek value through operating improvements, capital allocation and eventual liquidity events.

The central financial conclusion is that EA’s cash-generative business can support the proposed leverage, but the purchase valuation leaves limited room for disappointing operating performance. The consortium’s ultimate returns will depend more on sustained earnings growth and exit valuation than on leverage alone.
