I've been thinking about Nvidia's position in the artificial intelligence industry, particularly since the release of ChatGPT a few months ago. It's becoming increasingly clear that AI will require enormous amounts of computing power, and Nvidia happens to be one of the companies best positioned to provide it.

There's a good reason for that. Nvidia's graphics processing units, or GPUs, have become an important part of how AI models are developed. Its CUDA software platform also makes it easier for developers to use these processors, giving the company an advantage that goes beyond the hardware itself.

But there's something interesting about Nvidia's position. The more important AI becomes, the more money its largest customers have to spend on computing. And the more they spend, the greater their incentive to find alternatives.

I think this creates a problem for Nvidia over the longer term. Not because demand for its products will disappear, but because the very growth that makes its business attractive today could eventually make it harder to maintain its pricing power.

## The incentive to find an alternative

Nvidia's data-center business has already grown considerably. In the third quarter of its 2023 fiscal year, the company reported approximately $3.83 billion in data-center revenue, an increase of 31% from the previous year.

*Source: Nvidia quarterly earnings releases, 2022. Data-center revenue includes activities beyond generative AI.*

Some of Nvidia's most important customers are companies like Amazon, Google, and Microsoft. These businesses have enormous computing requirements, substantial financial resources, and engineering teams capable of developing their own technology.

Google has already developed its Tensor Processing Units, while Amazon has introduced processors called Trainium and Inferentia. In October 2022, Amazon announced that its Trainium-based cloud instances could reduce training costs by as much as 50% compared with certain GPU-based alternatives.

Of course, that's Amazon's own claim, and it doesn't mean its chips are better than Nvidia's in every situation. But it illustrates something important. Nvidia's customers aren't necessarily content to remain dependent on Nvidia forever.

Imagine a company spending $100 million every year on computing infrastructure. If developing its own processors costs $30 million but saves $20 million annually, the initial investment could pay for itself in about eighteen months.

That's a simplified example. In practice, developing chips involves considerable technical risk, software costs, and ongoing investment. But the underlying incentive is easy to understand. As spending increases, even relatively small savings become worth pursuing.

And if AI develops into an industry requiring hundreds of billions of dollars in computing infrastructure, I suspect these incentives will only become stronger.

## Not every AI application needs the same technology

There are broadly two stages involved. The first is training, where a model learns from large amounts of data. The second is inference, where that trained model is used to answer questions, generate text, or perform other tasks.

Training sophisticated models requires enormous computing power, and Nvidia's GPUs are particularly well suited to this work. Their flexibility, performance, and supporting software make them difficult to replace.

Inference can be different.

Once a model has been trained, some of the calculations involved in using it become more predictable. This creates opportunities for processors designed specifically for those tasks, potentially at a lower cost.

That doesn't mean specialized chips will replace GPUs altogether. Different applications have different requirements, and Nvidia may continue to offer the best overall solution for many of them.

If alternative processors become sufficiently efficient for even a portion of the inference market, customers will have more options. And having more options generally makes it harder for any one supplier to charge a substantial premium.

## Why replacing Nvidia won't be easy

Nvidia doesn't simply sell processors. It has spent years developing CUDA, a software platform that allows programmers to use its hardware efficiently. Developers have built tools, libraries, and applications around it, and moving those systems to another architecture can be expensive and time-consuming.

A company might discover that another processor is cheaper on paper, only to find that the engineering work required to switch makes the savings disappear. Nvidia also continues to improve its products, meaning competitors have to catch up with a moving target.

Its H100 GPUs, which began shipping last year, are a good example of that continued progress.

I don't expect these advantages to disappear anytime soon. In fact, they may allow Nvidia to remain the preferred supplier for the most demanding AI applications.

That distinction is important. Nvidia doesn't need every customer to consider switching for its pricing power to come under pressure. A few very large customers with credible alternatives could already change the economics of the market.

## What this could mean for Nvidia

I don't think any of this suggests that Nvidia's business is about to decline. Quite the opposite. AI could become an enormous source of revenue for the company over the coming years.

Nvidia could sell more processors, generate higher profits, and still find itself charging less for comparable computing performance as competition improves. Its margins might even remain healthy if manufacturing costs fall or its products become more efficient.

What concerns me is the assumption that today's competitive advantages will allow Nvidia to capture an unusually large share of AI spending indefinitely.

Over the next three to five years, I expect Nvidia's AI business to grow substantially. I also think there's a reasonable chance that specialized processors and internally developed chips will begin weakening its pricing premiums, particularly in applications where computing requirements become more standardized.

That depends on alternatives becoming genuinely competitive after accounting for performance, software, and operating costs. If Nvidia continues improving faster than its competitors, the pressure could take much longer to emerge.

The more valuable AI becomes, the more valuable it becomes for companies to find cheaper ways of running it. Some will continue buying Nvidia's products. Others will invest in alternatives. And even those that remain customers may gain negotiating power as the market develops.

Nvidia may end up being one of the biggest beneficiaries of artificial intelligence. I wouldn't be surprised if it does.

But I wouldn't assume that being essential to an industry today means being able to charge the same premium tomorrow.
