*Crude supply is recovering faster than refining capacity and petroleum-product trade. This divergence could prolong headline inflation pressure despite weaker demand.*

## Economic view

The Middle East energy shock has created a distinction between crude availability and refined-fuel supply. Although crude prices declined substantially during June, diesel, gasoline and other product markets remained constrained.

Our central expectation is that headline inflation will remain under pressure in H2 2026, even if crude benchmarks retreat from wartime highs. The argument is not that energy prices must produce persistent core inflation: restoring crude flows does not immediately restore refineries, product inventories or distribution networks. Different recovery speeds can delay relief to households and businesses.

## Crude recovery versus product shortages

The [IEA July Oil Market Report](https://www.iea.org/reports/oil-market-report-july-2026) estimates that global oil supply recovered by 4.1 million barrels per day in June to 98.8 million barrels per day, but remained approximately 9.4 million barrels per day below pre-war levels. Gulf exports recovered to 16.1 million barrels per day, compared with approximately 24 million before the conflict. Refinery operations recovered more slowly.

The OECD inventory decline includes government stock releases; it is not equivalent to a 62-million-barrel decline in commercial inventories. Emergency releases and alternative routes can cushion immediate shortages but do not replace damaged or unavailable refining and transport capacity.

## Inflation transmission

The [IMF July World Economic Outlook Update](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/weo/issues/2026/07/08/world-economic-outlook-update-july-2026) projects global headline inflation of 4.7% in 2026, compared with 4.1% in 2025, and global growth of 3.0%. This is consistent with an adverse supply shock but does not isolate how much of the revision comes from each energy-market disruption.

Households face direct changes in fuel and utility costs. Businesses face changes in freight, fuel and energy-intensive inputs, some of which may be passed to customers. The effect depends on expenditure weights, duration, exchange rates, taxes and government interventions.

A one-time increase in energy prices raises the price level but does not automatically generate persistent inflation. Repeated cost increases, delayed pass-through or broader wage and pricing adjustments would be required for sustained inflation pressure. The relevant question is whether physical disruption lasts long enough to generate repeated adjustments.

## Why lower crude prices may mislead

Crude is a refinery input. Lower crude prices need not produce equivalent declines in diesel or jet-fuel prices when processing capacity is constrained. The IEA reported refined-product cracks and margins at four-year highs in early July even as crude prices fell, suggesting bottlenecks had shifted partly toward processing and distribution.

For transport-intensive businesses, the delivered fuel price matters more than Brent alone. Crude benchmarks may therefore overstate the relief reaching the real economy. Monitoring diesel and jet-fuel spreads is more informative than watching crude in isolation.

## Growth and policy implications

Net energy importers face reduced purchasing power and potentially weaker trade balances. Manufacturing-intensive economies and those with limited fiscal capacity are especially exposed. Central banks cannot restore refineries through higher interest rates, but can influence demand and second-round inflation expectations. A response based only on headline energy inflation could unnecessarily weaken activity; sustained pass-through into wages and services would present a different policy problem.

## Outlook and monitoring

Our base case assumes improving crude availability but a slower recovery in refined-product supply. The key indicators are refinery throughput, product crack spreads, Gulf exports, commercial inventories and strategic releases.

**Exhibit 3. Forecast conditions**

| Development | Implication |
| --- | --- |
| Refinery throughput recovers rapidly | Weakens persistence thesis |
| Crude falls but product cracks stay elevated | Supports persistence thesis |
| Product inventories rebuild sustainably | Reduces near-term inflation risk |
| Renewed shipping disruption | Raises inflation and growth risks |
| Core inflation remains stable | Limits evidence of second-round effects |

Sustained normalization of refinery output, inventories and delivered fuel prices would weaken the forecast. Until then, falling crude benchmarks alone are insufficient evidence that the shock's inflationary consequences have ended.

**Central expectation:** Elevated headline inflation pressure persists through H2 2026, particularly in economies exposed to imported refined fuels and energy-intensive production.
