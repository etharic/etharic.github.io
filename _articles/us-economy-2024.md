I've been following the American economy's response to higher interest rates for some time now, and I find its resilience increasingly difficult to explain. The Federal Reserve has raised borrowing costs at one of the fastest rates in decades, inflation has fallen considerably from its peak, and yet the economy continues to grow. Unemployment remains low, businesses are still hiring, and consumers have shown surprisingly little willingness to cut back on spending.

The latest employment figures make this particularly interesting. The economy [added 336,000 jobs in September](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/empsit_10062023.htm), while unemployment remained at 3.8%. These are hardly the numbers one would expect from an economy that has spent the past eighteen months adjusting to substantially higher interest rates.

It's understandable why economists have become more optimistic about a soft landing, where inflation returns to more manageable levels without a recession. There is certainly evidence supporting that possibility, and I wouldn't dismiss it simply because previous tightening cycles have often ended badly.

Still, I find myself returning to a question that I don't think the recent economic data fully answers. Has the American economy genuinely adjusted to higher interest rates, or have we simply not seen all their consequences yet?

## The part of monetary policy we haven’t seen

One thing I've come to appreciate about monetary policy is how unevenly its effects are distributed over time. Interest rates can change almost overnight, but the financial decisions they influence often take months or even years to adjust.

Consider a business that borrowed money in 2020 at a relatively low fixed interest rate. Its borrowing costs don't necessarily change when the Federal Reserve raises rates. The problem only becomes apparent when the business needs additional financing or has to refinance its existing debt.

The same applies to households. Someone who secured a thirty-year fixed-rate mortgage a few years ago is largely protected from today's higher mortgage rates. Someone trying to purchase a home now faces a very different financial calculation.

This distinction matters because it helps explain why the economy has remained relatively strong despite the Federal Reserve [raising its benchmark interest-rate range to 5.25–5.50%](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20230920a.htm), compared with almost zero in early 2022.

Of course, this doesn't mean higher interest rates will inevitably cause a recession. Much of the adjustment may already have happened, and strong household incomes could continue offsetting the pressure from more expensive borrowing.

But I suspect there's still a meaningful amount of adjustment ahead of us, particularly for businesses that will eventually need to replace cheaper financing with more expensive debt.

## Where I think the real risk lies

What concerns me more than interest rates themselves is how businesses and banks are beginning to respond to them.

The Federal Reserve's [July survey of bank lending officers](https://www.federalreserve.gov/data/sloos/sloos-202307.htm) found that banks had tightened lending standards for several types of loans, including lending to businesses and commercial real estate borrowers. Banks also reported weaker demand for credit.

At first glance, this might not seem particularly alarming. Banks becoming more cautious when borrowing costs are high is hardly surprising, and businesses postponing investments can simply be a sensible response to uncertainty.

But I think the interaction between these decisions is worth paying attention to.

Imagine a small business considering whether to open another location. Higher interest rates make the investment less attractive, while stricter lending requirements make it more difficult to finance. The business may remain profitable and have plenty of customers, but management might reasonably decide to wait another year before expanding.

There is nothing particularly concerning about one business making that decision. The difficulty arises when thousands of businesses begin thinking the same way.

Lower investment means less spending on construction, equipment, and services. Companies supplying those goods and services may then become more cautious about hiring. If employment growth begins slowing, households could reduce their spending, creating further pressure on businesses.

I think this is a more plausible route to recession than the idea that higher interest rates will suddenly cause widespread financial distress. The economy doesn't need to experience a banking crisis for growth to weaken. A gradual reduction in spending and investment could be enough.

## The consumer is the difficult part of this prediction

The strongest argument against my view is the American consumer.

Despite inflation and higher borrowing costs, households have continued spending, supported by a strong labor market and rising incomes. It's difficult to argue convincingly for a recession while the largest component of the economy remains relatively healthy.

Households also entered this period with financial advantages that aren't always captured by historical comparisons. Many homeowners have fixed-rate mortgages, while pandemic-era savings provided additional support for consumption.

Those savings have been declining. [Research published by the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco in August](https://www.frbsf.org/research-and-insights/publications/economic-letter/2023/08/excess-no-more-dwindling-pandemic-savings/) estimated that a substantial portion of the excess savings accumulated during the pandemic had already been spent.

That doesn't mean consumers are about to run out of money. Aggregate savings estimates are uncertain, and households can continue spending as long as employment and incomes remain strong.

But it does make the labor market particularly important.

If businesses begin reducing investment and hiring, household income growth could eventually weaken. Consumers who have already drawn down much of their additional savings may then become more cautious, especially if borrowing remains expensive.

The difficulty is that this process depends on employment weakening in the first place. As long as businesses continue hiring and real incomes improve, consumer spending may remain strong enough to prevent a recession.

I think that's the central uncertainty in the outlook for next year.

## Why I’m still expecting a slowdown

There are other reasons for caution, including the continued inversion of the Treasury yield curve, which has [historically preceded several American recessions](https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/capital_markets/ycfaq.html). I wouldn't place too much weight on that indicator alone, since bond markets reflect many different expectations and historical patterns don't always repeat themselves.

What matters more to me is how several developments fit together. Borrowing costs have risen substantially, banks are becoming more selective about lending, and businesses face stronger incentives to postpone investment. Meanwhile, households may have less room to absorb financial pressure than they did earlier in the recovery.

None of these developments guarantees a recession. Taken together, though, I think they make a downturn more plausible than the recent strength of employment and spending might suggest.

My expectation is that the United States will enter a mild recession during 2024, most likely through a gradual weakening of business investment, hiring, and consumer spending rather than a sudden financial crisis.

I could be underestimating how well the economy will adapt. Inflation has already moderated considerably, and if it continues declining while employment remains strong, the Federal Reserve may have room to ease monetary policy before economic conditions deteriorate substantially.

That would be a welcome outcome, and there's a reasonable case for it.

Still, I think there's a difference between an economy that has remained resilient despite higher interest rates and one that has fully adjusted to them. The recent data demonstrate the former, but I'm not yet convinced they establish the latter.

For now, that's why I remain cautious about 2024.
